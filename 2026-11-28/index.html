Lead story · 🇦🇺 Australia · Online safety law

Australia passes the Digital Duty of Care, and it covers AI chatbots by name

New online safety laws have passed federal Parliament. Online services have to take more responsibility for protecting Australians from serious online harms. The Digital Duty of Care starts 12 months after Royal Assent. It applies to games, apps, social media platforms and AI chatbots. Services must protect users from seriously harmful material and conduct, and make sure their features and tools don't cause harm.

Platforms must also protect under-18s from harmful content, conduct and features, including pornography, eating-disorder material and online bullying. The eSafety Commissioner gets wider powers, including over cyberbullying and fake nude images. Penalties go up to 5% of global revenue or A$109.2 million, whichever is greater. Individuals and groups will also be able to sue for breaches. The department's notice doesn't say how chatbot features like emotional "companion" design will be judged. That will depend on rules and guidance still to come.

"It will require online services, including games, apps, social media platforms and AI chatbots to protect Australians from seriously harmful material and conduct." — Department of Infrastructure, Transport, Regional Development, Communications, Sport and the Arts

EnglandResearch

EEF launches three trials on how GenAI affects learning

On 27 Nov, England's Education Endowment Foundation (EEF) launched three randomised controlled trials, focused on "cognitive offloading", when students hand their thinking over to AI. The trials are: (1) Gemini's Guided Learning mode for Year 9 maths homework, over six weeks; (2) guided vs standard Copilot or Gemini vs no AI for Year 10 GCSE science revision, with an unaided test one to two weeks later; (3) Year 9 biology with and without AI, testing retention three weeks on and whether students know when to rely on AI. They run in spring and summer 2027.

"So far, most research has centred on how it can support teachers and there are significant gaps in our understanding of its impact on learning." — Prof. Becky Francis, EEF

South KoreaCurriculum

South Korea plans 1,500 AI-focused schools next year

Korea's Education Ministry plans to grow its AI-focused schools from 1,141 now to 1,500 in 2027 and 2,000 in 2028. These schools teach at least twice the usual computer-science hours. For example, middle schools teach at least 102 hours instead of 68, and high schools offer AI or CS every year. Examples include fifth graders learning smart farming and AI robot soccer, and high-school students evaluating AI-generated answers against the evidence.
